Four kinds of website.

A boxing club and an accountant should not get the same site. The first decision is the lane: how loud, how much moves, how fast it gets to the phone number. Every example is a full website for an invented business, running live.

When does a business need a loud website?

When being remembered matters more than being reassured. A boxing club or a tattoo studio is chosen on feel, so the site moves, the type hits and there is one thing the visitor will describe to a friend. Everything still works: the number, the booking and the address are one tap away.

What does a website for a film or video business look like?

Like a film. The visitor scrubs through the work by scrolling, the page carries a timecode, and nothing in it is a stock video: the frames are drawn in code, so the page weighs nothing and plays on a phone. The story reads even if you never scroll.

Why would a business choose a plain website?

Because its customers are buying trust, not excitement. A quiet page uses real typography, generous space and one accent, and moves exactly once. It is harder to make than a loud one, because nothing is hiding behind an animation.

What does a tradesperson’s website actually need?

The number, a form with three fields, proof you cover their street and a reason to ring you rather than the next result. These pages load in a blink, put a call bar under the thumb on a phone and answer the question the customer typed into Google. The only animation is the one that proves a point.
