I — The Scent

It opens cold. Wet stone, a rind of bergamot, the mineral shock of a wave that has just gone over you — then something moves underneath it and refuses to leave.

Sel Obscur was built backwards, from the base up. Birch tar first — the tarred-rope smell of a working harbour — then everything else arranged to keep it from shouting. Iris root cools it. Immortelle makes it sweet the way driftwood is sweet, which is barely.

It is not a beach. Beaches in perfumery are coconut and sunscreen and a kind of lying. This is the same coast in February: low sky, brown water, gorse burning on the headland, no one else out.

Eighteen months of trials. Forty-one versions. The one we bottled is the thirty-ninth.

II — The Object

01 — The glass

Nine millimetres thick at the base

Pressed in a small works outside Vannes, in a smoked flint glass that takes on the colour of whatever it is stood next to. The seam is hand-ground off. Every bottle is a fraction different.

02 — The collar

Oxidised silver, turned and unlacquered

It hazes to a flat pewter within a year of being handled, faster if you keep it in a bathroom. Salt air does it quicker still. That is intended. Polish it back if you must.

03 — The fill

50 ml, filled to the shoulder

Poured by hand in batches of forty, rested three weeks in the bottle before it ships. The number of your bottle is etched under the base with the date it was filled.

III — Sourcing

Seven materials,
and where they come from

Drag, scroll or arrow →

  1. 01Guérande, FR

    Fleur de sel

    Raked off the surface of the pans in the last hour of light, when the crust is still soft. We buy the second harvest, which carries more of the clay underneath.

  2. 02Corsica, FR

    Immortelle

    Distilled within six hours of cutting, on the same hillside. Beyond that the absolute goes flat and loses the burnt-honey edge we buy it for.

  3. 03Karelia, FI

    Birch tar

    Dry-distilled from bark in a sealed pit, the old way, by a co-operative of four families. Three-tenths of a percent in the formula. Any more and it swallows everything.

  4. 04Chianti, IT

    Iris pallida

    The rhizome is lifted, washed, and then left alone for three years so the irones can form. It is the most expensive thing in the bottle by a distance.

  5. 05Manabí, EC

    Ambrette seed

    A vegetal musk, which is the only kind we use. Cold-pressed, then held at low temperature for four months to drop the waxes out of it.

  6. 06Les Cayes, HT

    Vetiver, heavy cut

    Only the fraction that comes over late in the distillation — smoky, rooty, almost bitter. The bright top fraction is sold on; it has no place here.

  7. 07Ardèche, FR

    Cade oil

    Juniper wood, burned down in a kiln until it gives up a black, resinous oil. Rectified twice to strip the phenols that would otherwise stain fabric.

IV — The House

A house built on one stubborn idea

Camille Auber spent eleven years composing scents she was not allowed to sign, for houses that briefed her in adjectives. Fresh. Confident. Aspirational. In 2023 she was asked to make something that smelled like “the feeling of arriving somewhere expensive”, and she went home to Brittany instead.

MARÉE began in a rented room above a chandlery on the Quai Nord, with a bench, a rotary evaporator bought second-hand from a laboratory in Nantes, and a rule: no brief may be written in adjectives. Only in places, weather and hours of the day.

The first release took eighteen months, mostly because the birch tar kept winning. Forty-one submissions went to the bench. Nine people wore them for a fortnight each and reported back in plain sentences. Version thirty-nine was the one that made two of them say the same word without conferring: February.

Everything is composed, macerated, poured, labelled and boxed in the same room. There is no second product yet. There may not be one for a while — the second brief is written and it says only: a greenhouse in August, an hour after the watering.

Camille Auber, founder & nose
